# 1. Overview

<DefinitionCard term="Adjoint (Adjugate)" analogy="A 'partial undo' machine that overshoots by a factor of det(A) — divide by det(A) to get the true inverse.">
The **adjoint** (also called the **classical adjoint** or **adjugate**) of a square matrix $A$ is the transpose of the matrix of cofactors of $A$.
</DefinitionCard>

<DefinitionCard term="Inverse" analogy="The 'undo button' for a matrix transformation — apply A, then A⁻¹, and you get back exactly what you started with.">
The **inverse** of a square matrix $A$ is a unique matrix $A^{-1}$ such that $A \cdot A^{-1} = A^{-1} \cdot A = I$.
</DefinitionCard>

Together, the adjoint and inverse form the **algebraic machinery** that allows us to "divide" by a matrix — something that has no direct definition, but is achieved through the inverse operation.

## Why Was It Developed?

The concept emerged in the mid-19th century through the work of **Arthur Cayley** (1821–1895) and **James Joseph Sylvester** (1814–1897). Just as dividing both sides of $ax = b$ by $a$ gives $x = b/a$, the inverse provides the matrix analogue: $X = A^{-1}B$.

The adjoint formula ($A^{-1} = \text{adj}(A)/\det(A)$) connects cofactors, determinants, and the inverse into an elegant algebraic unity.

<EngineeringInsight>
Matrix inversion shows up everywhere in engineering — whenever you need to "undo" a transformation, solve a system, or recover original signals from measured data. If determinants tell you WHETHER a problem is solvable, the inverse tells you HOW to solve it.
</EngineeringInsight>

| Domain | Application |
|--------|-------------|
| Robotics & Automation | Jacobian inverse for velocity mapping, transformation matrix inverses |
| Control Systems | Transfer function: $G(s) = C(sI - A)^{-1}B + D$ |
| Signal Processing | Wiener filter: $w_{\text{opt}} = R^{-1}p$ |
| Circuit Analysis | Nodal/mesh analysis: $V = Y^{-1}I$ |
| Machine Learning | Normal equations: $(X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty$ |
| Optimization | Newton's method uses the Hessian inverse |

# 5. Intuitive Explanation

## 5.1 The "Undo" Button Analogy

**Imagine this...** You have a magical photo editing app. You apply a filter (matrix $A$). The **inverse** $A^{-1}$ is the **"undo" button** that perfectly reverses the filter: $A^{-1}(Ax) = x$.

**When does the undo button NOT exist?** When the filter converts color to black-and-white — color information is **permanently destroyed**. In matrix terms, this happens when $\det(A) = 0$ (singular). A singular matrix "crushes" space into a lower dimension.

**$A^{-1}$ exists if and only if $A$ doesn't destroy information, which happens precisely when $\det(A) \neq 0$.**

## 5.2 The Adjoint as a "Partial Undo"

The adjoint is like having the **right key, but it's too big** — it opens the lock but overshoots by a factor of $\det(A)$. To get the true inverse, divide by $\det(A)$:

<FormulaCard name="Fundamental Relation" description="The single most important result connecting cofactors, determinants, and the adjoint.">
$$A \cdot \text{adj}(A) = \det(A) \cdot I$$
</FormulaCard>

$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \cdot \text{adj}(A)$$

## 5.3 The Scalar Analogy

| Scalar | Matrix |
|--------|--------|
| "Inverse" of $a$ is $1/a$ | "Inverse" of $A$ is $A^{-1} = \text{adj}(A)/\det(A)$ |
| Exists when $a \neq 0$ | Exists when $\det(A) \neq 0$ |
| Solution: $x = b/a$ | Solution: $\mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b}$ |

## 5.4 Geometric Intuition

<Visualization id="transform-2d" matrix={[[3, 1], [2, 4]]} showGrid={true} showEigenvectors={false} />

- $|\det(A)|$ = area of the parallelogram the unit square maps to
- $\det(A) = 0$: square squished to a line → no inverse
- $\det(A) \neq 0$: transformation reversible → $A^{-1}$ maps back to unit square

## 5.5 The 2×2 Inverse: An Unforgettable Pattern

<FormulaCard name="2×2 Inverse" description="Swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal, divide by the determinant.">
$$\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}$$
</FormulaCard>

<MemoryTrick>
**"Swap-Negate-Divide"** — diagonal elements swap, off-diagonal elements negate, divide by $\det$.
</MemoryTrick>

## 5.6 What Happens If...

- **$A = I$?** $A^{-1} = I$, $\text{adj}(I) = I$
- **$A$ is diagonal?** Reciprocate each entry: $\text{diag}(a,b,c)^{-1} = \text{diag}(1/a,1/b,1/c)$
- **$A$ is orthogonal?** $A^{-1} = A^T$ — just transpose!
- **Invert twice?** $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$

## 5.7 Common Confusions

- **"Is $A^{-1}$ the same as $1/A$?"** No. $A^{-1}$ is a matrix satisfying $AA^{-1} = I$.
- **"Can rectangular matrices have inverses?"** Not classical. Use pseudo-inverse.
- **"Is $A^{-1}$ unique?"** Yes. Proof: if $B,C$ are both inverses, $B = BI = B(AC) = (BA)C = C$.
- **"Does $\text{adj}(A)$ exist for singular matrices?"** Yes, but the inverse doesn't.

<ConceptCheckpoint question="A 2×2 matrix A has det(A) = 0. What can you conclude?" options={["A⁻¹ = adj(A)", "A is invertible", "A is singular — no inverse exists", "A must be the zero matrix"]} correctIndex={2} explanation="det(A) = 0 means A is singular, so A⁻¹ does not exist. # 6. Formal Definitions

<DefinitionCard term="Minor" analogy="Delete a row and column, compute the determinant of what's left.">
The **minor** $M_{ij}$ of element $a_{ij}$ is the determinant of the $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ submatrix obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$.
</DefinitionCard>

<DefinitionCard term="Cofactor" analogy="The minor with a checkerboard sign.">
$C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} \cdot M_{ij}$
</DefinitionCard>

<DefinitionCard term="Adjoint (Adjugate)">
$\text{adj}(A) = [\text{cof}(A)]^T$ — the transpose of the cofactor matrix.

In element form: $[\text{adj}(A)]_{ij} = C_{ji}$
</DefinitionCard>

<Warning title="GATE Trap: Index Swap">
The $(i,j)$-entry of $\text{adj}(A)$ is the cofactor of $a_{ji}$ (indices swap due to transpose). This is the #1 source of errors.
</Warning>

<Warning title="Terminology Warning">
In GATE, "adjoint" = classical adjoint (transpose of cofactor matrix), NOT the conjugate transpose $A^\dagger$.
</Warning>

<DefinitionCard term="Inverse of a Matrix">
If $\det(A) \neq 0$, the inverse $A^{-1}$ is the unique matrix satisfying $AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I$, given by $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \cdot \text{adj}(A)$.
</DefinitionCard>

# 7. Mathematical Foundations

## 7.1 Construction of the Adjoint

**Step 1:** Compute minor $M_{ij}$ for each element. **Step 2:** Cofactor $C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}$. **Step 3:** Form cofactor matrix. **Step 4:** Transpose → $\text{adj}(A)$.

<MemoryTrick>
**2×2 Shortcut:** Swap diagonal, negate off-diagonal. Takes 2 seconds.
</MemoryTrick>

<ExampleCard title="3×3 Adjoint Computation" difficulty="intermediate">

**Problem:** $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & 5 \\ 1 & 0 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$

<div>

9 cofactors: $C_{11}=24$, $C_{12}=5$, $C_{13}=-4$, $C_{21}=-12$, $C_{22}=3$, $C_{23}=2$, $C_{31}=-2$, $C_{32}=-5$, $C_{33}=4$

$$\text{adj}(A) = \begin{bmatrix} 24 & -12 & -2 \\ 5 & 3 & -5 \\ -4 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

</div>
</ExampleCard>

## 7.2 Proof: A · adj(A) = det(A) · I

$(i,j)$-entry of $A \cdot \text{adj}(A)$: $\sum_k a_{ik} C_{jk}$

- **$i = j$:** cofactor expansion of $\det(A)$ along row $i$ → $\det(A)$ ✓
- **$i \neq j$:** determinant of matrix with two identical rows → $0$ ✓

## 7.3 Inverse Formula Derivation

From $A \cdot \text{adj}(A) = \det(A) \cdot I$, divide by $\det(A)$ (when $\neq 0$):

<FormulaCard name="Inverse via Adjoint" description="The adjoint formula for computing A⁻¹.">
$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \cdot \text{adj}(A)$$
</FormulaCard>

## 7.4 Uniqueness

If $B$ and $C$ are both inverses: $B = BI = B(AC) = (BA)C = IC = C$. ∎

## 7.5 Gauss-Jordan Method

Form $[A|I]$, row-reduce to $[I|A^{-1}]$.

<ExampleCard title="3×3 Gauss-Jordan" difficulty="intermediate">

**Problem:** Find inverse of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & 5 \\ 1 & 0 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$

<div>

$[A|I] \xrightarrow{R_3-R_1} \xrightarrow{R_3+\frac{1}{2}R_2} \xrightarrow{\text{back-sub}}$

$\det(A) = 22$

$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{22}\begin{bmatrix} 24 & -12 & -2 \\ 5 & 3 & -5 \\ -4 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

Both methods (adjoint and Gauss-Jordan) agree ✓

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<ExamTip exam="GATE">
For square matrices, verifying ONE of $AA^{-1}=I$ or $A^{-1}A=I$ is sufficient — the other follows automatically.
</ExamTip>

# 8. Theoretical Concepts

## 8.1 Conditions for Invertibility

| # | Condition |
|---|-----------|
| 1 | $\det(A) \neq 0$ |
| 2 | $\text{rank}(A) = n$ |
| 3 | Rows linearly independent |
| 4 | Columns linearly independent |
| 5 | $Ax = 0$ has only trivial solution |
| 6 | $Ax = b$ has unique solution for every $b$ |
| 7 | Row-equivalent to $I_n$ |
| 8 | Product of elementary matrices |
| 9 | $0$ is NOT an eigenvalue |
| 10 | $A^T$ is invertible |

All 10 are **equivalent** — if any one fails, ALL fail.

## 8.2 Special Matrix Inverses

| Matrix Type | Inverse |
|-------------|---------|
| Diagonal $\text{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_n)$ | $\text{diag}(1/d_1,\ldots,1/d_n)$ |
| Orthogonal $Q$ | $Q^{-1} = Q^T$ |
| Symmetric $A$ | $A^{-1}$ is also symmetric |
| Upper triangular | Upper triangular (reciprocal diagonals) |
| Lower triangular | Lower triangular (reciprocal diagonals) |
| Rotation $R$ | $R^{-1} = R^T$ |

## 8.3 Key Inverse Properties

- $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ (order reverses)
- $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$
- $(kA)^{-1} = \frac{1}{k}A^{-1}$
- $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det(A)$
- $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$
- $A^{-n} = (A^{-1})^n = (A^n)^{-1}$

<MemoryTrick>
**"Socks and Shoes"** for $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$: put on socks then shoes → remove shoes then socks.
</MemoryTrick>

# 10. Important Properties

## Adjoint Properties

| # | Property | Formula |
|---|----------|---------|
| 1 | Fundamental relation | $A \cdot \text{adj}(A) = \det(A) \cdot I$ |
| 2 | Adjoint of transpose | $\text{adj}(A^T) = [\text{adj}(A)]^T$ |
| 3 | Adjoint of product | $\text{adj}(AB) = \text{adj}(B) \cdot \text{adj}(A)$ |
| 4 | Scalar multiple | $\text{adj}(kA) = k^{n-1} \cdot \text{adj}(A)$ |
| 5 | Double adjoint | $\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A)) = [\det(A)]^{n-2} \cdot A$ |
| 6 | Determinant | $\det(\text{adj}(A)) = [\det(A)]^{n-1}$ |
| 7 | Adjoint of inverse | $\text{adj}(A^{-1}) = A/\det(A)$ |

<FormulaCard name="det(adj(A))" description="Most frequently tested adjoint property in GATE.">
$$\det(\text{adj}(A)) = [\det(A)]^{n-1}$$
</FormulaCard>

<FormulaCard name="adj(adj(A))" description="Double-adjoint formula — a GATE favorite.">
$$\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A)) = [\det(A)]^{n-2} \cdot A$$
</FormulaCard>

### Rank of adj(A)

| Condition | rank(adj(A)) |
|-----------|:---:|
| rank(A) = $n$ | $n$ |
| rank(A) = $n-1$ | $1$ |
| rank(A) $< n-1$ | $0$ |

<MemoryTrick>
**"N-1-0 Rule"** for rank of adjoint: Full rank → $n$; one less → $1$; anything less → $0$.
</MemoryTrick>

## Inverse Properties

| # | Property | Formula |
|---|----------|---------|
| 1 | Definition | $AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I$ |
| 2 | Inverse of inverse | $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$ |
| 3 | Inverse of transpose | $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$ |
| 4 | Inverse of product | $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ |
| 5 | Scalar multiple | $(kA)^{-1} = \frac{1}{k}A^{-1}$ |
| 6 | Determinant | $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det(A)$ |
| 7 | Powers | $A^{-n} = (A^{-1})^n$ |

# 11. Algorithms

**Algorithm 11.1: Adjoint Formula** — Compute det, all cofactors, transpose, divide. Complexity: $O(n!)$.

**Algorithm 11.2: Gauss-Jordan** — Form $[A|I]$, row-reduce to $[I|A^{-1}]$. Complexity: $O(n^3)$.

**Algorithm 11.3: 2×2 Shortcut** — Swap diagonal, negate off-diagonal, divide by det. Time: ~30 seconds.

<ExamTip exam="GATE">
$2\times2$: always swap-negate-divide. $3\times3$: prefer Gauss-Jordan (faster, fewer errors). Special matrices: exploit structure.
</ExamTip>

# 13. Worked Examples

<ExampleCard title="13.1 — 2×2 Adjoint and Inverse" difficulty="beginner">

**Problem:** Find adjoint and inverse of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 7 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$.

<div>

$\det(A) = 15 - 14 = 1$. $\text{adj}(A) = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -7 \\ -2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

$A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -7 \\ -2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. When $\det = 1$, inverse = adjoint!

Verify: $AA^{-1} = I$ ✓

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<ExampleCard title="13.2 — 3×3 Inverse" difficulty="intermediate">

**Problem:** Find $A^{-1}$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ 3 & 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

<div>

$\det(A) = 12 + 10 - 2 = 20$

$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{20}\begin{bmatrix} 6 & 3 & -1 \\ -10 & 5 & 5 \\ -2 & -1 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$

Quick check: Row 1 of $A$ × Col 1 of $A^{-1}$: $\frac{1}{20}(12+10-2) = 1$ ✓

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<ExampleCard title="13.3 — Properties of Adjoint (GATE)" difficulty="advanced">

**Problem:** $A$ is $3\times3$, $\det(A)=5$. Find: (a) $\det(\text{adj}(A))$, (b) $\det(\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A)))$, (c) $\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A))$

<div>

(a) $[\det(A)]^{n-1} = 5^2 = 25$

(b) Let $B = \text{adj}(A)$, $\det(B)=25$. $\det(\text{adj}(B)) = 25^2 = 625$

(c) $[\det(A)]^{n-2} \cdot A = 5A$

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<ExampleCard title="13.4 — Matrix Equation" difficulty="intermediate">

**Problem:** $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, $AX = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}$. Find $X$.

<div>

$\det(A) = -2$, $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 3/2 & -1/2 \end{bmatrix}$

$X = A^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}5\\11\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}$. Verify: $A\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}5\\11\end{bmatrix}$ ✓

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<ExampleCard title="13.5 — Cayley-Hamilton Trick" difficulty="advanced">

**Problem:** $A^2 - 5A + 6I = O$. Find $A^{-1}$.

<div>

Multiply by $A^{-1}$: $A - 5I + 6A^{-1} = O$

$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}(5I - A)$$

Eigenvalues are 2 and 3 (both nonzero), so $A$ is invertible ✓

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<ExampleCard title="13.6 — Robot Transformation Inverse" difficulty="advanced">

**Problem:** $T = \begin{bmatrix} 0&-1&3 \\ 1&0&2 \\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix}$. Find $T^{-1}$.

<div>

$R^T = \begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{bmatrix}$, $-R^Td = \begin{bmatrix}-2\\3\end{bmatrix}$

$$T^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0&1&-2 \\ -1&0&3 \\ 0&0&1 \end{bmatrix}$$

In robotics, NEVER use general inversion for transformation matrices — the special structure makes it trivial.

</div>
</ExampleCard>

# 14. Common Mistakes

<Warning title="Forgetting the Transpose">
$\text{adj}(A) = [\text{cof}(A)]^T$, NOT $\text{cof}(A)$ itself.
</Warning>

<Warning title="Wrong Order in Product Inverse">
$(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$, NOT $A^{-1}B^{-1}$.
</Warning>

| # | Mistake | Correct |
|---|---------|---------|
| 1 | Forgetting to transpose cofactor matrix | $\text{adj}(A) = [\text{cof}(A)]^T$ |
| 2 | Wrong cofactor sign | Use $(-1)^{i+j}$ checkerboard |
| 3 | Wrong submatrix for minor | Delete entire row $i$ AND column $j$ |
| 4 | Inverting singular matrix | Check $\det(A) \neq 0$ first |
| 5 | $(AB)^{-1} = A^{-1}B^{-1}$ | It's $B^{-1}A^{-1}$ |
| 6 | Confusing adj with conjugate transpose | GATE: adj = classical adjoint |
| 7 | $\det(kA) = k\det(A)$ | It's $k^n\det(A)$ |
| 8 | All triangular matrices invertible | Need ALL diagonal entries $\neq 0$ |

# 16. Formula Sheet

<FormulaCard name="Adjoint Formulas" description="Quick reference.">
- $\text{adj}(A) = [\text{cof}(A)]^T$
- $A \cdot \text{adj}(A) = \det(A) \cdot I$
- $\det(\text{adj}(A)) = [\det(A)]^{n-1}$
- $\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A)) = [\det(A)]^{n-2} \cdot A$
- $\text{adj}(kA) = k^{n-1} \cdot \text{adj}(A)$
- $\text{adj}(AB) = \text{adj}(B) \cdot \text{adj}(A)$
</FormulaCard>

<FormulaCard name="Inverse Formulas" description="Quick reference.">
- $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \cdot \text{adj}(A)$
- $\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{bmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{bmatrix}$
- $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$
- $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$
- $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det(A)$
</FormulaCard>

**Special:** Orthogonal: $Q^{-1}=Q^T$ | Diagonal: reciprocate | Symmetric: $A^{-1}$ symmetric | Triangular: same type

# 17. Quick Revision Notes

1. $\text{adj}(A) = [\text{cof}(A)]^T$ — always transpose!
2. $A \cdot \text{adj}(A) = \det(A) \cdot I$ — the fundamental relation
3. $A^{-1} = \text{adj}(A)/\det(A)$ — exists only when $\det(A) \neq 0$
4. 2×2 inverse: swap diagonal, negate off-diagonal, divide by det
5. $\det(\text{adj}(A)) = [\det(A)]^{n-1}$ — GATE favorite
6. $\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A)) = [\det(A)]^{n-2} \cdot A$ — double-adjoint
7. $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ — order reverses
8. Gauss-Jordan: $[A|I] \to [I|A^{-1}]$
9. Cayley-Hamilton: if $p(A)=O$, multiply by $A^{-1}$ to find $A^{-1}$
10. Invertible $\iff$ $\det \neq 0$ $\iff$ full rank $\iff$ no zero eigenvalue

## Assertion-Reason (Q21–Q25)

**Q21.** A: If $\det(A)=0$, adj(A) may or may not be zero. R: rank(adj(A))=1 when rank(A)=$n-1$, 0 when rank(A)$<n-1$.

**Q22.** A: Inverse of symmetric is symmetric. R: $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1} = A^{-1}$.

**Q23.** A: If $AB=O$ and $A$ invertible, then $B=O$. R: $A^{-1}(AB) = A^{-1}O = O$, so $B=O$.

# 20. Detailed Solutions

**S1.** The transpose makes the fundamental relation $A\cdot\text{adj}(A)=\det(A)\cdot I$ work: diagonal entries reproduce det via cofactor expansion; off-diagonal entries give zero (repeated rows).

**S2.** All 10 conditions characterize the same property: the transformation is bijective.

**S3.** $\det=0$ means the unit square collapses to a line (area=0). Multiple inputs map to same output → irreversible.

**S4.** Classical adjoint = $[\text{cof}(A)]^T$. Hermitian adjoint = $\bar{A}^T$. GATE means classical.

**S5.** $Q^TQ=I$ (definition) $\implies Q^{-1}=Q^T$. Also $QQ^T=I$ follows since $\det(Q)\neq 0$.

**S6.** rank(A)=3=$n-1$ (where $n=4$), so rank(adj(A))=**1**.

**S7.** No classical inverse. Left inverse: $(A^TA)^{-1}A^T$. Right inverse: $A^T(AA^T)^{-1}$. Pseudo-inverse $A^+$ always exists.

**S8.** Socks-shoes: last applied = first undone. Proof: $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1})=A(BB^{-1})A^{-1}=I$.

**S9.** Yes. $\det(A)=\prod\lambda_i\neq 0$, so invertible.

**S10.** Adjoint: $O(n\cdot n!)$. Gauss-Jordan: $O(n^3)$. For $n=10$: millions vs thousands of operations.

**S11.** $\det=5$, adj $=\begin{bmatrix}4&-3\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}$, $A^{-1}=\frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix}4&-3\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}$.

**S12.** $\det=3$, $A^{-1}=\frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}1&4&-2\\-2&-5&4\\1&-2&1\end{bmatrix}$.

**S13.** Upper triangular, $\det=1$. $A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&0\\0&1&-1\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}$.

**S14.** $\det=1$, $A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}7&-2\\-3&1\end{bmatrix}$.

**S15.** (a) $4^2=16$ (b) $8\cdot\frac{1}{4}=2$ (c) $\det(2A)=32$, $\det(\text{adj}(2A))=32^2=1024$.

**S16.** $\det=30$, $A^{-1}=\text{diag}(1/3,1/5,1/2)$, $\text{adj}(A)=\text{diag}(10,6,15)$.

**S17.** $\det=1$, $X=\begin{bmatrix}3\\-2\end{bmatrix}$.

**S18.** $A^{-1}=\frac{1}{6}(A^2-6A+11I)$. Eigenvalues: 1,2,3.

**S19.** $\det=6-2k=0 \implies k=3$.

**S20.** $\det=\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$, $A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}=A^T$.

**S21.** (a) — Both true, R explains A.

**S22.** (a) — Both true, R explains A.

**S23.** (a) — Both true, R explains A.

**S24.** (a) — Both true, R explains A.

**S25.** (d) — A false (need nonzero diagonal), R true.

**S26.** (a),(b),(c),(d) — all correct.

**S27.** (b),(c).

**S28.** (a),(c),(d).

**S29.** (a),(b),(c).

**S30.** (b),(c).

**S31.** $\text{adj}(A^T)=3(A^T)^{-1}$. $B=6(A^TA)^{-1}$. $\det(B)=216/9=24$.

**S32.** $\det(A)=2$. $\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A))=4A$. $\det=4^4\cdot 2=512$.

**S33.** $A^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-a\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, $A^n=\begin{bmatrix}1&na\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$, $(A^n)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&-na\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$.

**S34.** $\det(AB^{-1})=2/3$. $\det(\text{adj}(AB^{-1}))=(2/3)^2=4/9$.

**S35.** If invertible: $A^{-1}A^2=A^{-1}A \implies A=I$, contradiction. So not invertible. $\det(A)^2=\det(A) \implies \det(A)=0$.
