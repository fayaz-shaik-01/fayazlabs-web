# 1. Overview

## 1.1 What is a Determinant?

<DefinitionCard term="Determinant" analogy="A single number that acts like a medical blood test for a matrix — it reveals invertibility, area/volume scaling, and orientation all at once.">
A **determinant** is a single **number** computed from a square matrix. It takes an entire $n \times n$ matrix and compresses it into one scalar value.
</DefinitionCard>

$$\det(A) \quad \text{or} \quad |A| \quad \text{— a single number computed from matrix } A$$

<FormulaCard name="2×2 Determinant" description="Product of main diagonal minus product of anti-diagonal.">
$$A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \implies \det(A) = ad - bc$$
</FormulaCard>

For example:

$$\det\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = 3(4) - 2(1) = 12 - 2 = 10$$

That's it — one number. But this one number tells you an extraordinary amount about the matrix.

## 1.2 Why Were Determinants Developed?

Determinants were invented to answer a fundamental question: **Does a system of linear equations have a unique solution?**

Consider: $2x + 3y = 7$ and $x - y = 1$.

The coefficient matrix is $A = \begin{bmatrix}2&3\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}$, and $\det(A) = 2(-1)-3(1) = -5 \neq 0$.

Since the determinant is non-zero, the system has a **unique solution**. If the determinant were zero, the system would either have no solution or infinitely many solutions.

**Historical note:** The concept was developed independently by Leibniz (1693) and the Japanese mathematician Seki Takakazu (1683), both in the context of solving systems of equations. The word "determinant" was coined by Gauss in 1801 because it **determines** whether a system has a unique solution.

## 1.3 What Does the Determinant Tell You?

The determinant encodes multiple pieces of information in one number. Think of it like a **medical blood test** — a single blood sample can tell a doctor about your blood sugar, cholesterol, liver function, and more. Similarly, a single determinant value tells you about a matrix's invertibility, the behavior of the system of equations it represents, the geometric scaling it produces, and whether it preserves orientation.

Here's the remarkable thing: if someone gives you a square matrix and asks "Is it invertible? Does the system have a unique solution? Are the rows independent? How much does it scale area?" — the answer to ALL these questions comes from **one** number: the determinant.

Let's break down exactly what information it carries:

| Property | What det tells you |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Invertibility** | $\det(A) \neq 0 \iff A$ is invertible |
| **System of equations** | $\det(A) \neq 0 \iff$ unique solution |
| **Linear independence** | $\det(A) \neq 0 \iff$ rows (or columns) are linearly independent |
| **Area/Volume scaling** | $\lvert\det(A)\rvert$ = factor by which $A$ scales areas (2D) or volumes (3D) |
| **Orientation** | $\det(A) > 0$: orientation preserved; $\det(A) < 0$: orientation reversed |
| **Singularity** | $\det(A) = 0 \iff A$ is singular (non-invertible) |

## 1.4 Why Are Determinants Important in Engineering?

<EngineeringInsight>
Determinants show up in almost every branch of engineering, often as the **go/no-go test** for whether a system works properly. Whenever an engineer needs to know if a system of equations is solvable, if a robot can move freely, if a control system can be steered, or if signals are independent — they compute a determinant.
</EngineeringInsight>

| Domain | Application |
|--------|-------------|
| Robotics | Checking if a robot configuration is at a **singularity** (det of Jacobian = 0) |
| Control Systems | Checking **controllability/observability** (det of controllability matrix) |
| Circuit Analysis | Solving node/mesh equations using Cramer's rule |
| Structural Analysis | Checking stability of structures (det of stiffness matrix) |
| Computer Graphics | Determining if a transformation preserves orientation |
| Machine Learning | Multivariate Gaussian: $\det(\Sigma)$ appears in the normalization constant |
| Signal Processing | Checking if signals are linearly independent |

<RealWorldExample title="Robotics: Jacobian Singularity">
The Jacobian matrix $J$ of a robot maps joint velocities to end-effector velocities. When $\det(J) = 0$, the robot is at a **singularity** — it loses the ability to move in certain directions. Every robotics engineer must compute determinants to identify and avoid singularities.
</RealWorldExample>

# 5. Intuitive Explanation

Before we dive into formulas and computations, let's spend significant time building a **deep, intuitive understanding** of what a determinant really is and why it matters. If you understand the intuition well, the formulas become natural rather than something to memorize.

## 5.1 The Big Idea: A Determinant Is a "Scale Factor"

**Imagine this...** You have a rubber sheet (like a trampoline surface) with a perfect 1×1 square drawn on it. Now imagine you grab the corners and stretch, squeeze, rotate, or shear the rubber sheet according to some rule (that rule is the matrix). After the transformation, that perfect square has become some kind of parallelogram.

The determinant tells you exactly **how much the area changed**. If the original square had area 1, and after the transformation the parallelogram has area 6, then the determinant is 6 (or -6 if the orientation flipped).

This is the single most important intuition: **the determinant measures how much a linear transformation scales area (in 2D) or volume (in 3D).**

<Visualization id="transform-2d" matrix={[[2, 0], [0, 3]]} showGrid={true} showEigenvectors={false} />

## 5.2 Analogy: The Area Machine

Let's make this concrete with a $2 \times 2$ matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}$.

Take the **unit square** — the square with corners at $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,1)$. Its area is exactly 1 square unit.

Now apply the matrix $A$ to every corner. The four corners move to new positions, and the unit square transforms into a **parallelogram** (or possibly collapses into a line or a point).

**The absolute value of the determinant equals the area of that parallelogram.**

$$\lvert\det(A)\rvert = \text{area of the parallelogram}$$

But the determinant also carries a **sign**, and the sign tells you about orientation:

- If $\det(A) > 0$: the orientation is **preserved**. Counterclockwise stays counterclockwise.
- If $\det(A) < 0$: the orientation is **flipped**. Counterclockwise becomes clockwise.
- If $\det(A) = 0$: the parallelogram **collapses** to a line (or a point) — area is 0. The matrix is singular.

**Imagine this...** Think of the matrix as an instruction to a photocopier. A determinant of 2 means the copy is twice the size. A determinant of 0.5 means the copy is half the size. A determinant of -1 means the copy is the same size but mirror-flipped. And a determinant of 0? The copier has jammed — the entire image has been squashed into a line.

**Concrete examples:**

- $A = \begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{bmatrix}$: $\det(A) = 6$. Stretches horizontally by 2 and vertically by 3. Area = $2 \times 3 = 6$.
- $A = \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$: $\det(A) = 1$. **Shears** the square but doesn't change the area.
- $A = \begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}$: $\det(A) = 0$. Row 2 = 2× Row 1. Collapses to a line. Singular.
- $A = \begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$: $\det(A) = -1$. Swaps x and y (reflection). Area preserved, orientation flipped.

## 5.3 Extending to 3D: Volume

In 3D, a $3 \times 3$ matrix transforms the **unit cube** into a **parallelepiped**.

$$\lvert\det(A)\rvert = \text{volume of the parallelepiped}$$

**Concrete 3D example:** $A = \begin{bmatrix}2&0&0\\0&3&0\\0&0&4\end{bmatrix}$. This stretches space by 2 along x, 3 along y, 4 along z. Volume = $2 \times 3 \times 4 = 24 = \det(A)$.

<EngineeringInsight>
In robotics, the quantity $\lvert\det(J)\rvert$ — the **manipulability index** — measures how efficiently the robot can move. Engineers plot it across the workspace to identify and avoid singularities.
</EngineeringInsight>

## 5.4 Analogy: The Invertibility Detector

- $\det(A) \neq 0$: Matrix is "healthy" — invertible, transformation can be undone.
- $\det(A) = 0$: Matrix is "broken" — singular, information permanently lost.

## 5.5 Analogy: The Recipe Test (Linear Independence)

**Imagine this...** Suppose you have 3 cake recipes arranged as matrix rows. If Recipe 2 = 2× Recipe 1, the determinant is **zero** — you only have 2 unique recipes. Nonzero determinant confirms all recipes are genuinely different (linearly independent).

## 5.6 Intuition for Key Properties

**Why does swapping two rows flip the sign?** Swapping two vectors defining a parallelogram preserves area but flips orientation.

**Why does adding a multiple of one row to another leave det unchanged?** It shears the parallelogram without changing area.

**Why is $\det(AB) = \det(A) \cdot \det(B)$?** If $A$ scales area by $\det(A)$ and $B$ by $\det(B)$, the combined scaling is the product.

**Why is $\det(kA) = k^n \det(A)$?** Every row gets multiplied by $k$. With $n$ rows, that's $k^n$.

<ConceptCheckpoint question="A 3×3 matrix A has det(A) = 5. What is det(2A)?" options={["10", "20", "40", "80"]} correctIndex={2} explanation="det(kA) = k^n · det(A). Here k=2, n=3, so det(2A) = 2³ × 5 = 8 × 5 = 40." />

## 5.7 The "Zero or Nonzero" Perspective

For most GATE problems, you just need to know whether det is **zero or nonzero**:
- Invertible? (nonzero → yes)
- Unique solution? (nonzero → yes)
- Independent rows? (nonzero → yes)
- Robot singularity? (zero → yes)

## 5.8 Common Confusions Cleared Up

**"Is the determinant a matrix?"** No — it's a scalar.

**"Can non-square matrices have determinants?"** No — only $n \times n$.

**"Does $\det(A) = 0$ mean $A$ is the zero matrix?"** No. $\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}$ has $\det = 0$ but is not zero.

## 5.9 Summary of Key Intuitions

| Intuition | Summary |
|-----------|---------|
| **Scale factor** | $\lvert\det(A)\rvert$ = how much $A$ scales area (2D) or volume (3D) |
| **Sign** | $\det > 0$: orientation preserved; $\det < 0$: orientation flipped |
| **Zero** | $\det = 0$: space collapses, matrix is singular |
| **Invertibility** | $\det \neq 0 \iff$ matrix is invertible |
| **Independence** | $\det \neq 0 \iff$ rows/columns are linearly independent |

Most questions test whether you can use **properties** to avoid brute-force computation. The properties in Section 8 are your primary weapons.
</ExamTip>

# 6. Formal Definitions

<DefinitionCard term="Minor" analogy="Delete a row and column from a spreadsheet and compute the determinant of what's left.">
The **minor** $M_{ij}$ of element $a_{ij}$ in an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is the determinant of the $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ matrix obtained by **deleting row $i$ and column $j$** from $A$.
</DefinitionCard>

**Example:** For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$:

$M_{11} = \det\begin{bmatrix}5&6\\8&9\end{bmatrix} = 45 - 48 = -3$

$M_{12} = \det\begin{bmatrix}4&6\\7&9\end{bmatrix} = 36 - 42 = -6$

$M_{23} = \det\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\7&8\end{bmatrix} = 8 - 14 = -6$

<DefinitionCard term="Cofactor" analogy="The minor with a checkerboard sign — like alternating black and white squares on a chessboard.">
The **cofactor** $C_{ij}$ of element $a_{ij}$ is: $C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} \cdot M_{ij}$
</DefinitionCard>

<MemoryTrick>
**"Even = Plus, Odd = Minus"** — if $i+j$ is even, the cofactor sign is $+$; if odd, it's $-$.
</MemoryTrick>

**Continuing the example:**
- $C_{11} = (+1)(-3) = -3$
- $C_{12} = (-1)(-6) = +6$
- $C_{23} = (-1)(-6) = +6$

<DefinitionCard term="Determinant via Cofactor Expansion">
Expand along any row $i$: $\det(A) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij} \cdot C_{ij}$

Or along any column $j$: $\det(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{ij} \cdot C_{ij}$

The result is the same regardless of which row or column you choose. Expand along the row/column with the **most zeros**.
</DefinitionCard>

# 7. Mathematical Foundations

<FormulaCard name="1×1 Determinant" description="Just the element itself — the base case.">
$$\det[a] = a$$
</FormulaCard>

<FormulaCard name="2×2 Determinant" description="Main diagonal product minus anti-diagonal product.">
$$\det\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = ad - bc$$
</FormulaCard>

<MemoryTrick>
```
[ a   b ]       det = a×d − b×c
[ c   d ]             ↘     ↗
                    main   anti
```
</MemoryTrick>

**Worked examples:**

$$\det\begin{bmatrix}3&7\\1&5\end{bmatrix} = 15 - 7 = 8$$

$$\det\begin{bmatrix}4&6\\2&3\end{bmatrix} = 12 - 12 = 0 \quad \text{(singular!)}$$

$$\det\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{bmatrix} = 0 + 1 = 1 \quad \text{(rotation matrix)}$$

## 7.3 Sarrus' Rule (3×3 Only)

Step 1: Write matrix, repeat first two columns to the right.

Step 2: Add three downward diagonal products. Subtract three upward diagonal products.

<MemoryTrick>
**"Down Plus, Up Minus"** — downward diagonals are added, upward diagonals are subtracted.
</MemoryTrick>

<ExampleCard title="3×3 Determinant — Sarrus' Rule" difficulty="beginner">

**Problem:** $\det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$

<div>

```
1  2  3 | 1  2
4  5  6 | 4  5
7  8  9 | 7  8
```

Down (↘): $1(5)(9) + 2(6)(7) + 3(4)(8) = 45 + 84 + 96 = 225$

Up (↗): $3(5)(7) + 1(6)(8) + 2(4)(9) = 105 + 48 + 72 = 225$

$\det = 225 - 225 = 0$ — Singular! (Rows in arithmetic progression.)

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<Warning title="Sarrus' Rule — Size Limitation">
Sarrus' Rule works ONLY for $3 \times 3$ matrices. For $4 \times 4$ or larger, use cofactor expansion or row reduction.
</Warning>

## 7.4 Cofactor Expansion (3×3 Example)

Expanding $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$ along Row 1:

$\det(A) = 1(-3) + 2(6) + 3(-3) = -3 + 12 - 9 = 0$ ✓

## 7.5 Choosing the Best Row/Column

<ExamTip exam="GATE">
Expand along the row or column with the most zeros. Each zero eliminates an entire minor computation.
</ExamTip>

## 7.6 Triangular and Diagonal Matrices

<FormulaCard name="Triangular Matrix Determinant" description="Product of diagonal elements.">
$$\det(\text{triangular}) = a_{11} \cdot a_{22} \cdot \ldots \cdot a_{nn} = \prod_{i=1}^n a_{ii}$$
</FormulaCard>

**Corollaries:** $\det(I) = 1$, $\det(kI) = k^n$.

## 7.7 4×4 Example

<ExampleCard title="4×4 Determinant" difficulty="intermediate">

**Problem:** $\det\begin{bmatrix} 1&0&2&0 \\ 0&3&0&0 \\ 2&0&1&0 \\ 0&0&0&4 \end{bmatrix}$

<div>

Expand along Row 2 (three zeros): $\det = 3 \cdot C_{22}$

$C_{22} = \det\begin{bmatrix}1&2&0\\2&1&0\\0&0&4\end{bmatrix}$. Expand along Col 3: $= 4 \cdot \det\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix} = 4(-3) = -12$

$\det = 3(-12) = -36$

</div>
</ExampleCard>

# 8. Theoretical Concepts

## 8.1 Properties of Determinants

<FormulaCard name="Transpose Rule" description="Swapping rows and columns doesn't change the determinant.">
$$\det(A^T) = \det(A)$$
</FormulaCard>

**Property 2: Row/Column Swap → Sign Change**

$$R_i \leftrightarrow R_j \implies \det_{\text{new}} = -\det_{\text{old}}$$

**Consequence:** Two identical rows → $\det = 0$.

**Property 3: Scalar Multiple of a Row**

Multiplying one row by $k$ multiplies det by $k$.

<FormulaCard name="Scalar Multiple Rule" description="Multiplying the ENTIRE n×n matrix by k gives k^n, not k.">
$$\det(kA) = k^n \det(A)$$
</FormulaCard>

<Warning title="The #1 GATE Trap">
$\det(2A) \neq 2\det(A)$ for $n > 1$. For $3 \times 3$: $\det(2A) = 8\det(A)$.
</Warning>

**Property 4:** Row of all zeros → $\det = 0$.

**Property 5:** Proportional rows → $\det = 0$.

**Property 6: Row Addition** — Adding $k \times$ one row to another does **NOT** change det.

<FormulaCard name="Product Rule" description="Determinant of a product = product of determinants.">
$$\det(AB) = \det(A) \cdot \det(B)$$
</FormulaCard>

**Consequences:** $\det(A^n) = [\det(A)]^n$, $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det(A)$, $\det(A^T A) = [\det(A)]^2$.

<FormulaCard name="Adjoint Rule" description="Determinant of the adjoint.">
$$\det(\text{adj}(A)) = [\det(A)]^{n-1}$$
</FormulaCard>

### Summary Table of Row/Column Operations

| Operation | Effect on Determinant |
|-----------|----------------------|
| Swap two rows/columns | Multiplies by $-1$ |
| Multiply one row/column by $k$ | Multiplies by $k$ |
| Add $k \times$ row to another row | **No change** |
| Row of all zeros | Det = 0 |
| Two identical/proportional rows | Det = 0 |

<ConceptCheckpoint question="Which row operation does NOT change the determinant?" options={["Swapping two rows", "Adding 3× row 1 to row 2", "Multiplying a row by 5", "All of these change it"]} correctIndex={1} explanation="Adding a multiple of one row to another does NOT change the determinant." />

## 8.2 Special Determinant Values

**Orthogonal:** $\det(Q) = \pm 1$

**Skew-symmetric (odd order):** $\det(A) = 0$ — GATE favorite!

**Idempotent ($A^2=A$):** $\det(A) \in \{'{'}0, 1\}$

**Nilpotent ($A^k=O$):** $\det(A) = 0$

**Involutory ($A^2=I$):** $\det(A) = \pm 1$

## 8.4 Cramer's Rule

<FormulaCard name="Cramer's Rule" description="Solve Ax=b by replacing columns and computing determinants.">
$$x_j = \frac{\det(A_j)}{\det(A)} \quad \text{where } A_j \text{ has column } j \text{ replaced by } \mathbf{'{'}b{'}'}$$
</FormulaCard>

## 8.5 Vandermonde Determinant

<FormulaCard name="Vandermonde (3×3)" description="Each row has successive powers of a variable.">
$$\det\begin{bmatrix} 1&a&a^2 \\ 1&b&b^2 \\ 1&c&c^2 \end{bmatrix} = (b-a)(c-a)(c-b)$$
</FormulaCard>

General: $\det(V) = \prod_{1 \leq i < j \leq n} (x_j - x_i)$. Non-zero iff all $x_i$ distinct.

## 8.6 Block Diagonal Determinant

$$\det\begin{bmatrix}A&C\\O&B\end{bmatrix} = \det(A) \cdot \det(B)$$

## 8.7 Circulant Determinant

For $3 \times 3$ circulant: $\det(C) = c_0^3 + c_1^3 + c_2^3 - 3c_0 c_1 c_2$

```
       / area=|det|
      /________
     /        /
    /________/
  O        v₁ = (3,0)
```

$A = \begin{bmatrix}3&1\\0&2\end{bmatrix}$, $\det(A) = 6$. Area = 6.

# 11. Algorithms / Procedures

**Algorithm 11.1:** $2 \times 2$: $ad - bc$. Time: $O(1)$.

**Algorithm 11.2:** $3 \times 3$ Sarrus: 6 multiplications. Time: $O(1)$.

**Algorithm 11.3:** $n \times n$ Cofactor Expansion: Time $O(n!)$. Fine for GATE ($n \leq 4$).

**Algorithm 11.4:** Row Reduction to triangular: Time $O(n^3)$. Used by computers.

<ExamTip exam="GATE">
For $2\times2$ and $3\times3$, use direct formulas. For $4\times4$, use row operations to create zeros, then expand.
</ExamTip>

# 12. Engineering Applications

<RealWorldExample title="Robotics: Jacobian Singularity Detection">
The Jacobian $J$ maps joint velocities to end-effector velocity: $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = J(\mathbf{q}) \dot{\mathbf{q}}$. When $\det(J) = 0$, the robot is at a singularity.
</RealWorldExample>

<RealWorldExample title="Control Systems: Controllability">
A system $(A, B)$ is controllable iff $\det[B \mid AB \mid A^2B \mid \cdots \mid A^{n-1}B] \neq 0$.
</RealWorldExample>

<RealWorldExample title="Circuit Analysis: Cramer's Rule">
After setting up nodal equations $G\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{I}$, Cramer's rule finds each node voltage: $V_k = \det(G_k)/\det(G)$.
</RealWorldExample>

# 13. Worked Examples

<ExampleCard title="Example 13.1 — 2×2 Determinant" difficulty="beginner">

**Problem:** Find $\det\begin{bmatrix}7&3\\2&5\end{bmatrix}$.

<div>

$\det = 7(5) - 3(2) = 35 - 6 = 29$.

Since $\det \neq 0$, this matrix is **invertible**.

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<ExampleCard title="Example 13.2 — 3×3 with Sarrus" difficulty="beginner">

**Problem:** $\det\begin{bmatrix}2&1&3\\0&-1&2\\4&0&1\end{bmatrix}$

<div>

Down: $2(-1)(1) + 1(2)(4) + 3(0)(0) = -2 + 8 + 0 = 6$

Up: $3(-1)(4) + 2(2)(0) + 1(0)(1) = -12 + 0 + 0 = -12$

$\det = 6 - (-12) = 18$

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<ExampleCard title="Example 13.3 — Using Properties" difficulty="intermediate">

**Problem:** $A$ is $3 \times 3$, $\det(A) = 4$. Find: (a) $\det(2A)$ (b) $\det(A^{-1})$ (c) $\det(A^3)$ (d) $\det(A^T A)$

<div>

(a) $\det(2A) = 2^3 \cdot 4 = 32$

(b) $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/4$

(c) $\det(A^3) = 4^3 = 64$

(d) $\det(A^TA) = 4 \times 4 = 16$

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<ExampleCard title="Example 13.4 — Row Reduction" difficulty="intermediate">

**Problem:** $\det\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\2&5&7\\3&7&10\end{bmatrix}$

<div>

$R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1$, $R_3 \to R_3 - 3R_1$:

$$\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\\0&1&1\\0&1&1\end{bmatrix}$$

Rows 2 and 3 identical → $\det = 0$.

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<ExampleCard title="Example 13.5 — 4×4 Determinant" difficulty="advanced">

**Problem:** $\det\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&3\\2&1&0&0\\0&3&2&0\\0&0&1&4\end{bmatrix}$

<div>

Expand along Row 1: $= 1 \cdot C_{11} + 3 \cdot C_{14}$

$C_{11} = \det\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\3&2&0\\0&1&4\end{bmatrix} = 1 \times 2 \times 4 = 8$ (triangular)

$C_{14} = (-1)^{1+4}\det\begin{bmatrix}2&1&0\\0&3&2\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} = (-1)(6) = -6$ (triangular)

$\det = 1(8) + 3(-6) = 8 - 18 = -10$

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<ExampleCard title="Example 13.6 — Cramer's Rule (3×3)" difficulty="advanced">

**Problem:** Solve $x + y + z = 6$, $x - y + z = 2$, $2x + y - z = 1$

<div>

$D = 6$, $D_x = 6$, $D_y = 12$, $D_z = 18$

$x = 1$, $y = 2$, $z = 3$

Verify: $1+2+3=6$ ✓, $1-2+3=2$ ✓, $2+2-3=1$ ✓.

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<ExampleCard title="Example 13.7 — Robot Singularity Check" difficulty="advanced">

**Problem:** 2-link planar robot with $l_1 = l_2 = 1$. Find when $\det(J) = 0$.

<div>

After simplification: $\det(J) = \sin\theta_2$

Singularity when $\theta_2 = 0$ (fully stretched) or $\theta_2 = \pi$ (fully folded).

</div>
</ExampleCard>

<ExampleCard title="Example 13.9 — Common Trap" difficulty="beginner">

**Problem:** Student claims $\det(2A) = 2 \times 5 = 10$ for $3 \times 3$ $A$ with $\det(A) = 5$.

<div>

**Wrong!** $\det(2A) = 2^3 \times 5 = 40$, not 10. The exponent is the matrix size.

</div>
</ExampleCard>

# 14. Common Mistakes

<Warning title="det(kA) = k·det(A)">
Each of $n$ rows gets multiplied by $k$. Correct: $\det(kA) = k^n\det(A)$.
</Warning>

<Warning title="det(A+B) = det(A) + det(B)">
Determinant is NOT linear over matrix addition. No simple formula; compute directly.
</Warning>

<Warning title="Using Sarrus for 4×4">
Sarrus works ONLY for $3\times3$. Use cofactor expansion or row reduction for larger matrices.
</Warning>

<Warning title="Forgetting sign in cofactor">
Sign depends on position: $C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}$.
</Warning>

<Warning title="Row swap without tracking sign">
Each swap flips the sign. Track carefully!
</Warning>

| # | Mistake | Correct |
|---|---------|---------|
| 1 | $\det(kA) = k\det(A)$ | $k^n\det(A)$ |
| 2 | $\det(A+B) = \det(A)+\det(B)$ | No simple formula |
| 3 | $\det(A^T) = -\det(A)$ | $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$ |
| 4 | Forgetting cofactor sign | $(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}$ |
| 5 | Sarrus for $4\times4$ | Cofactor expansion only |
| 6 | $\det=0 \implies A=O$ | Singular, not zero |

# 16. Formula Sheet

<FormulaCard name="2×2" description="Basic formula">
$$\det\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix} = ad-bc$$
</FormulaCard>

<FormulaCard name="Cofactor" description="Minor with sign">
$$C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}M_{ij}$$
</FormulaCard>

<FormulaCard name="Key Identities" description="Most-tested properties">
- $\det(kA) = k^n\det(A)$
- $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$
- $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det(A)$
- $\det(A^m) = [\det(A)]^m$
- $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$
- $\det(\text{adj}A) = [\det(A)]^{n-1}$
</FormulaCard>

**Special matrices:** Orthogonal: $\pm1$ | Skew-sym (odd): $0$ | Idempotent: $0$ or $1$ | Nilpotent: $0$

**Vandermonde (3×3):** $(b-a)(c-a)(c-b)$

**Cramer:** $x_j = \det(A_j)/\det(A)$

# 17. Quick Revision Notes

1. Determinant = single number from a square matrix. $2\times2$: $ad-bc$.
2. $\det \neq 0 \iff$ invertible. $\det = 0 \iff$ singular.
3. Triangular/diagonal det = product of diagonal entries.
4. $\det(kA) = k^n\det(A)$ — the #1 GATE trap.
5. $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$. $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det(A)$.
6. Row swap → sign flips. Row addition → no change.
7. Expand along row/column with most zeros.
8. Sarrus works ONLY for $3\times3$.
9. $\lvert\det(A)\rvert$ = area (2D) or volume (3D) scaling factor.
10. Cramer's rule: $x_j = \det(A_j)/\det(A)$.

**S6.** Swap: $-12$. Row addition: no change. Final: $-12$.

**S7.** $(-1)^5 = -1$.

# 24. Exam Strategy

<ExamTip exam="GATE">
**How Determinants Are Tested:**
- **1-mark MCQ:** $\det(kA)$ trap, property-based true/false, det of special matrices
- **2-mark NAT:** Compute $3\times3$ or $4\times4$ det, Cramer's rule
- **Embedded:** Part of eigenvalue problems ($\det(A-\lambda I)=0$)
</ExamTip>

<ExamTip exam="GATE">
**Top 5 Shortcuts:**
1. Triangular → product of diagonal.
2. $\det(kA) = k^n\det(A)$ — always check $n$.
3. Expand along row/column with most zeros.
4. $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$ — break compound problems into pieces.
5. Identical/proportional rows → $\det = 0$ instantly.
</ExamTip>

<Warning title="Traps to Avoid">
- $\det(kA) \neq k\det(A)$
- $\det(A+B) \neq \det(A)+\det(B)$
- Sarrus ONLY for $3\times3$
- Track sign changes during row swaps
- Cofactor sign: $(-1)^{i+j}$
</Warning>
